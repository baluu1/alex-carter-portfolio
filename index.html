Three treatments.

Not much of this job happens with a camera in your hands. It happens earlier — deciding what a place is supposed to feel like, then working backwards to the frames that prove it.

Below are three self-initiated concepts: the light key, the sequence, and the one decision that holds each set together.

Concept development — treatment plates and shot notes, not final photography.

01

The Hoxton Morning

Boutique hospitality / Lifestyle

Role
Photography & Visual Direction
Light key
North window, 07:10 – 09:30
Frames
Six, sequenced

A quiet morning inside a contemporary London boutique hotel.

The problem

Hotel photography sells a room. It almost never sells a morning. Everything is made, folded and lit an hour before anyone would actually be awake.

The decision

Photograph the moments between actions. The pause before the cup is picked up, the sheet still holding a shape. Nobody performs hospitality.

02

After Hours / Soho

Restaurant / Nightlife editorial

Role
Photography & Creative Direction
Light key
Practical warm → street sodium
Runs
21:00 – 01:00

A Soho restaurant moving from dinner into late evening.

The problem

Restaurant photography documents dishes. It rarely tells you what the room sounds like at eleven, which is the only thing anyone is actually booking.

The decision

Shoot the reflections rather than the subjects. Glass, chrome, wet pavement — the room appears twice in most frames, and never quite the same way.

03

Form & Motion

Fashion / Editorial

Role
Photography & Art Direction
Light key
Hard midday, concrete bounce
Setup
One wall, one source

A minimal independent fashion campaign built on movement and silhouette.

The problem

Motion usually costs you the garment. The clothes go soft and the shape goes with them, and you end up with an atmosphere nobody can buy.

The decision

Fix the body sharp and let only the fabric blur. One wall, one source, no styling tricks — so the difference between frames is the movement and nothing else.

About

A beautiful photograph is easy to admire and easy to forget.

I started photographing in London in 2021 — street work first, then portraits, mostly because it gave me a reason to be out in the city at the right time of day.

Commercial work arrived through hospitality, and somewhere in there the job quietly changed. A single good frame is easy to make and easy to lose. A set of images that agree with each other is what actually changes how a place feels.

So now the work starts earlier: deciding what deserves attention. Where the light comes from, how people move through a room, which three details somebody still remembers a week later — and then making sure forty frames say the same thing.

I use available light wherever it's possible. I'd rather wait twenty minutes for a room to be right than fix it afterwards.
